# This channel's style: an animated short where the viewer is the hero

This is not an explainer with pictures. It is a short film: one person (the viewer, "you") lives through the story scene by scene, other people talk to you, and you talk back. Every idea arrives as something that happens to you, never as a lecture.

## Plain English, always

The viewer is watching on a phone, maybe in their second language. Write so a 12-year-old follows every sentence the first time they hear it.
- Short sentences: about 8 words on average. Mix tiny ones ("You blink.") with longer ones.
- Everyday words. If an idea has a name (inflation, a monopoly, a patent), SHOW it first and name it after: "On Monday bread costs ten. On Friday it costs a hundred. That has a name: inflation."
- Never: deficit, denomination, nominal, real terms, expectations, fiscal, monetary, liquidity, transactions, institutions, stakeholders, infrastructure. Say what they mean in a scene instead ("the government spends more than it has, so it prints more").
- One idea per beat. A number is always tied to something you can picture: not "inflation hit 231 million percent" alone, but "a loaf that cost one dollar in January costs millions by July".
- Never talk about the video, its facts or its sources in the narration. Sources go in `claims`.

## The recipe

1. COLD OPEN AT THE WORST MOMENT (beats 1-6). Drop "you" into the most striking moment of the story, years in: who you are, where, what is in your hand, what just went wrong. Then one beat that makes it worse, and: "To understand how, we need to go back."
2. GIVE "YOU" A LIFE. You are a specific ordinary person inside the story: the young engineer at Kodak, a teacher in Harare, the shop owner, the first employee. You have a job, a boss, a family, money in a jar. Keep it the same person all the way through.
3. EVERY CHAPTER IS A SCENE. One or two narrator beats set it up (where, when, what you want), then two to five spoken lines between characters, then the narrator lands what it meant. About one beat in four is spoken by a character. Facts the viewer must know come out of the scene: a price tag, a news report, a boss's order, an argument.
4. REACTIONS ARE LINES. You answer in a few words where a real person would ("Using what?", "Twenty thousand?"). Deadpan beats comedy.
5. A RUNNING GAG AND A CALLBACK. Invent one object or habit that keeps coming back and means more each time, and one short line said early that returns at the end with a new meaning. They must come from THIS story. Never reuse the examples below.
6. THE BIG NUMBERS ON CARDS. Real numbers still go on `stat` and `duo` cards with sources, three to six of them. Dialogue never carries a number that is not true.
7. THE ENDING. The narrator reveals the real names and the real place, says the lesson in one short line, then asks the viewer one question about their own life that fits THIS story.

## What a spoken line is

A spoken line (`speaker` set to a character) is words that person says OUT LOUD to someone in the scene, in their own voice: "Could we build a camera with no film?" Narration about you, even in the second person ("You walk to the shop."), is the narrator: leave `speaker` as narrator. Characters are unnamed roles (boss, exec_a, woman, anchor), never a real person's name. A line may repeat what was really said or asked, or be ordinary talk that claims no fact ("Just see if it works."). Never invent a fact, number or decision through dialogue; the description will say the dialogue is dramatized.

## A worked example (Kodak, 1975): match its rhythm, never its lines

narrator: January nineteenth, twenty twelve. You're sixty-one, retired, and half asleep in front of the evening news.
anchor (news object): Breaking news tonight. Eastman Kodak, the hundred-and-thirty-one-year-old photography giant, has filed for bankruptcy.
narrator: You sit up.
narrator: Kodak. The company where you spent most of your working life.
narrator: And the craziest part? You're not even surprised.
narrator: Because thirty-seven years ago, you built the thing that killed it. And they told you to keep it quiet.
narrator: To understand how, we need to go back.
narrator: Nineteen seventy-four. You're twenty-three, fresh out of engineering school, and you've just landed a job at Kodak.
narrator: One afternoon, your boss stops by your desk. The whole conversation takes about thirty seconds.
boss: Hey. Could we build a camera using those new electronic light sensors?
narrator: You blink.
you: Using what?
boss: Just see if it works.
narrator: And he walks off. That's it. That's the whole assignment.
... (a year of building; the first photo: static where a face should be; a colleague says "Needs work.")
narrator: Nineteen seventy-six. A windowless conference room. A long table full of very important people.
exec_a: Why would anyone want to look at their pictures on a television?
exec_c: When could a customer actually buy this?
you: Honestly? Fifteen, maybe twenty years.
narrator: The whole room relaxes. Twenty years is forever.
... (the napkin: Kodak makes its money on film; the camera goes into a drawer; the drawer swallows the warning report too)
narrator: In real life, that young engineer was named Steve Sasson. He built the first digital camera at twenty-four. And Kodak put it in a drawer.

Notice: the sentences are short, every fact is a moment, the viewer is always somewhere doing something, and the scenes are exchanges.
